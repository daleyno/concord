The school on the ridge

A history of Concord School, South Park, Seattle

Concord School stands on a ridge above the Duwamish River in South Park, at 723 South Concord Street. Designed by district architect Edgar Blair and opened in January 1914, its three stories of red and buff brick have served the neighborhood’s children for more than a century.

South Park has changed a great deal around it. The river was straightened into a shipping channel, farmland gave way to industry, a freeway was built along the hill and, from the 1970s, the neighborhood became home to a large Latino community. The school changed too. It was enlarged, renovated, designated a city landmark and, in 2009, began teaching in both Spanish and English.

This account follows Concord from its predecessor at the foot of the hill to the dual-language school of today. It draws on district records, the city’s historic sites survey, the 1999 renovation drawings and photographs held by the Seattle Public Schools Archives and the Museum of History & Industry.

01 Chapter 1 Before the hill

Below the hill

South Park, 1850s to 1914

“Many explored it on tippy rafts and had to explain why they were tardy and dripping wet.”

On the slough beside South Park School. Building for Learning: Seattle Public School Histories, 1862-2022.

For thousands of years the Duwamish people lived along the river that gives the valley its name, fishing its waters and building cedar longhouses on its banks.

American settlers began claiming land in the area in the 1850s. In 1889 a farm on the river was platted as the town of South Park, though it grew slowly; industry favored neighbouring Georgetown and South Park remained largely agricultural.

By the early 1900s many of its farmers were Italian and Japanese immigrants who grew vegetables on the river flats. After 1907 they sold much of their produce directly at the new Pike Place Market. South Park incorporated as a city in 1905 and two years later voted, 186 to 36, to be annexed by Seattle.

South Park School

The neighborhood’s first school, South Park School, was an eight-room, two-story wooden building on Twelfth Avenue South, at the bottom of the hill. It had opened by 1902 and became part of the Seattle School District with annexation. A slough beside the school was a known temptation for students, some of whom arrived late and wet after exploring it on rafts.

By 1909 the school was overcrowded. That year the board purchased a site on Concord Street between Seventh and Eighth Avenues South, and in 1912 it voted to build a fireproof brick school there. The new building was named Concord School after the street it faced; the name was approved on February 24, 1913.

The move

On January 25, 1914 between 200 and 300 students cleared their desks at South Park School and carried their books and supplies up the hill. As they approached the new building, the first streetcar crossed the new bridge over the Duwamish on Eighth Avenue South.

The grounds were unfinished. At the first recess the schoolyard was deep mud, and students who played in it were sent home to clean up. Others who left the grounds lost their way in the ferns and hazelnut bushes that still covered the hillside. The principal, F. C. Jackson, who had come from South Park School, set new rules soon afterwards.

The old building remained in use as an annex of Concord until September 1932. It was demolished in 1938, and the site is now occupied by the South Park Community Center.

Concord School was designed by Edgar Blair, architect to the Seattle School District.

Born in Des Moines, Iowa, in 1871, Blair studied architecture at Columbia University and worked as a draftsman for McKim, Mead & White in New York before opening his own practice in Washington, DC. He moved to Seattle in 1906, joined the school board’s staff as head draftsman by 1909 and served as district architect from 1910 to 1918, working on about 32 schools.

Seattle’s population was growing rapidly, and the district needed durable, fire-resistant buildings that could be built quickly in new neighborhoods. Blair’s response was a series of standard designs in brick. Concord belongs to one such group: the district’s history records it as identical to McGilvra School in Madison Park and McDonald School near Green Lake, both completed within months of Concord.

The design

The building is a three-story rectangle under a low hipped roof. Its walls are red brick, banded with courses of buff brick, and a deep wooden cornice with modillions runs below the eaves. Tall sash windows are grouped in threes and fours to light the classrooms. The entrance projects from the main facade in pale brick and cast stone, with the school’s name carved in the lintel and a decorated surround framing the doors.

The city’s historic sites survey classifies the style as Beaux Arts Neoclassical; the school district describes it as Colonial Revival. In either case the effect relies less on ornament than on proportion, the rhythm of the window bays and the contrast of the two brick colors.

Cost and siting

The school cost $80,460 to build and enrolled about 240 students in its first year. It was placed on the most prominent ridge in the neighborhood, visible from the farms below. The city’s survey notes that its plan, windows and cladding remain intact and that it contributes to South Park’s historic identity.

Blair returned to private practice in 1919 and died in Seattle in 1924.

03 Chapter 3 The class portraits

On the front steps

The class portraits, 1914 to 1997

For much of the twentieth century Concord’s classes were photographed each year on the stair of the main entrance.

The prints in the district archive are numbered in white ink and dated by hand, and together they form a record of the neighborhood’s children across eight decades.

The earliest surviving portrait shows the entire school assembled at once. Students fill the stair and the ground on either side of it, the youngest seated in front, the oldest standing on the stair walls, with members of the baseball team in their lettered jerseys among them. The portraits of the 1930s show single classes: boys in overalls and lace-up boots, girls in cotton print dresses and, in winter, heavy coats.

South Park at that time was a community of small farms and houses, home to families of Italian, Japanese and other backgrounds. Japanese American farmers in the Duwamish and Green River valleys supplied a large share of the produce sold at Pike Place Market, although state law prevented them from owning land.

In 1942, under Executive Order 9066, Japanese American families in the region were forcibly removed and incarcerated. Many did not return. The class portraits from the preceding years include some of their children.

The same stair, in color

The class portraits continued into the 1990s. The setting had not changed: the photographer still worked at the main entrance, and students still stood on the stair between the same stone walls, the smallest in front. The prints were now in color, mounted on card with the school year, the teacher’s name and the names of every student.

The 1996-97 school year was a significant one for Concord. That fall it became an ESL and bilingual center, with about 17 percent of its students receiving bilingual services. By then South Park was home to a large Latino community.

In 1999 the school closed for renovation. When it reopened in September 2000, the entrance and its stair, designated a city landmark the year before, had been preserved unchanged.

04 Chapter 4 Inside the school

The lunchroom and the blackboard

Inside the school, 1940s to 1990

A photograph from the Seattle Post-Intelligencer collection shows Concord’s lunchroom at a busy midday: long wooden tables and benches, glass milk bottles and plates of food, with staff in aprons serving at the far end of the room.

Enrollment rose sharply after the Second World War. It reached its peak of 516 students in 1958-59, and by 1960 six portable classrooms stood on the grounds. In 1971 the district added a gymnasium and multipurpose room, designed by Shavey & Schmidt, and the number of portables was reduced to three.

By the early 1970s a large mural covered the lunchroom’s end wall: concentric rings, clouds and stars, plants and fruit, and at its center a sun with a face. In one photograph of the period, a hand-lettered sign beside the flag warns that the paint is still wet.

The 1938 blackboard

In 1990 workers removing a chalkboard to install bulletin boards found an older blackboard behind it. The sixth-grade class of June 1938 had decorated it with three colored-chalk drawings of scenes from Norway, Portugal and Italy.

The six artists signed beneath their pictures, and their 20 classmates signed in lists between them. The discovery led to a reunion of the teacher, Mary Luch Bleakney, and several of her former students.

05 Chapter 5 Renovation

Repair, not replacement

Landmark status and the 1999 drawings

By the 1990s the building needed substantial work.

Seismic upgrades were carried out in 1992, but the building systems were ageing and the school needed more space. As with many schools of its period, the district faced a choice between renovation and replacement.

On February 18, 1998 the City of Seattle designated the 1913 exterior and the first-floor entrance stairwell as a landmark. The renovation that followed was funded by the district’s first Building Excellence levy and designed by the Tsang Partnership.

The record drawings, dated February 1999, set out what would be removed and what would be kept. Interior partitions, finishes and ceilings were to be demolished. Structural beams and columns, concrete floors, stairs and masonry walls were to remain. The three roof ventilators were to be refurbished, the masonry cleaned and decayed wood sash replaced to match the original.

A new classroom wing and gymnasium were built beside the original in banded masonry block. Students spent the 1999-2000 school year at the E. C. Hughes building and returned in September 2000. The district also purchased the remaining houses on the block, bringing the whole of it into school ownership.

06 Chapter 6 The school today

Five figures

The emblem and the school today

Two copies of Concord’s emblem hang beside the main entrance, below the name carved in 1913.

It is a bold letter C divided into five segments, each holding a cut-paper figure: a bird, a branching plant, a winding river, a creature and an open book.

The school publishes no key to the emblem. Read against what happens inside the building today, though, each figure finds a counterpart. In 2018 Concord International School taught 365 students from kindergarten to fifth grade, about 60 percent of them Spanish speakers.

  1. 1

    Bird Pollinator garden

    An S-shaped garden of native plants, funded in 2017 by the King Conservation District and the City of Seattle, was planned to attract bees, butterflies and hummingbirds and to give students a place for outdoor science.

  2. 2

    Branch STEAM and the maker space

    Students learn the engineering design process (ask, imagine, plan, create, improve) and measure and build with paper, cardboard and wood.

  3. 3

    River The Green Team

    In the school’s environmental stewardship program, older students sort food waste at breakfast and lunch, and every classroom recycles. Duwamish Opportunity Fund grants helped renew the front entry.

  4. 4

    Book A bilingual library

    A growing collection of authentic Spanish-language books and works in translation supports reading in both languages of the dual-language program.

  5. 5

    Creature Music and art

    Students sing, dance and play xylophones, djembes, tubanos, bongos and ukuleles. Art lessons study artists from many cultures; one mosaic project took a reclining puma as its guide.
