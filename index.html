The school on the ridge

A history of Concord School, South Park, Seattle

In the 1920s Concord School fielded a baseball team. Its players wore the school’s name across their jerseys and were photographed on the grounds in front of the main entrance, beneath the words Concord School carved in stone. The building behind them was then barely ten years old: three storeys of red and buff brick on a ridge above the Duwamish River, built by the Seattle School District in 1913 and opened in January 1914.

A century later the building still stands and still serves as an elementary school. In that time South Park has changed a great deal. The river was straightened into a shipping channel, farmland gave way to industry, a freeway was built along the hill and, from the 1970s, the neighbourhood became home to a large Latino community.

This account follows the school from its predecessor at the foot of the hill to the dual-language school of today. It draws on district records, the city’s historic sites survey, the 1999 renovation drawings and photographs held by the Seattle Public Schools Archives and the Museum of History & Industry.

01 Chapter 1 Before the hill

Below the hill

South Park, 1850s to 1914

“Many explored it on tippy rafts and had to explain why they were tardy and dripping wet.”

On the slough beside South Park School. Building for Learning: Seattle Public School Histories, 1862-2022.

For thousands of years the Duwamish people lived along the river that gives the valley its name, fishing its waters and building cedar longhouses on its banks.

American settlers began claiming land in the area in the 1850s. In 1889 a farm on the river was platted as the town of South Park, though it grew slowly; industry favoured neighbouring Georgetown and South Park remained largely agricultural.

By the early 1900s many of its farmers were Italian and Japanese immigrants who grew vegetables on the river flats. After 1907 they sold much of their produce directly at the new Pike Place Market. South Park incorporated as a city in 1905 and two years later voted, 186 to 36, to be annexed by Seattle.

South Park School

The neighbourhood’s first school, South Park School, was an eight-room, two-storey wooden building on Twelfth Avenue South, at the bottom of the hill. It had opened by 1902 and became part of the Seattle School District with annexation. A slough beside the school was a known temptation for pupils, some of whom arrived late and wet after exploring it on rafts.

By 1909 the school was overcrowded. That year the board purchased a site on Concord Street between Seventh and Eighth Avenues South, and in 1912 it voted to build a fireproof brick school there. The new building was named Concord School after the street it faced; the name was approved on 24 February 1913.

The move

On 25 January 1914 between 200 and 300 pupils cleared their desks at South Park School and carried their books and supplies up the hill. As they approached the new building, the first streetcar crossed the new bridge over the Duwamish on Eighth Avenue South.

The grounds were unfinished. At the first recess the schoolyard was deep mud, and pupils who played in it were sent home to clean up. Others who left the grounds lost their way in the ferns and hazelnut bushes that still covered the hillside. The principal, F. C. Jackson, who had come from South Park School, set new rules soon afterwards.

The old building remained in use as an annex of Concord until September 1932. It was demolished in 1938, and the site is now occupied by the South Park Community Center.

Concord School was designed by Edgar Blair, architect to the Seattle School District.

Born in Des Moines, Iowa, in 1871, Blair studied architecture at Columbia University and worked as a draughtsman for McKim, Mead & White in New York before opening his own practice in Washington, DC. He moved to Seattle in 1906, joined the school board’s staff as head draughtsman by 1909 and served as district architect from 1910 to 1918, working on about 32 schools.

Seattle’s population was growing rapidly, and the district needed durable, fire-resistant buildings that could be built quickly in new neighbourhoods. Blair’s response was a series of standard designs in brick. Concord belongs to one such group: the district’s history records it as identical to McGilvra School in Madison Park and McDonald School near Green Lake, both completed within months of Concord.

The design

The building is a three-storey rectangle under a low hipped roof. Its walls are red brick, banded with courses of buff brick, and a deep wooden cornice with modillions runs below the eaves. Tall sash windows are grouped in threes and fours to light the classrooms. The entrance projects from the main façade in pale brick and cast stone, with the school’s name carved in the lintel and a decorated surround framing the doors.

The city’s historic sites survey classifies the style as Beaux Arts Neoclassical; the school district describes it as Colonial Revival. In either case the effect relies less on ornament than on proportion, the rhythm of the window bays and the contrast of the two brick colours.

Cost and siting

The school cost $80,460 to build and enrolled about 240 pupils in its first year. It was placed on the most prominent ridge in the neighbourhood, visible from the farms below. The city’s survey notes that its plan, windows and cladding remain intact and that it contributes to South Park’s historic identity.

Blair returned to private practice in 1919 and died in Seattle in 1924.

03 Chapter 3 The class portraits

On the front steps

The class portraits, 1914 to 1997

For much of the twentieth century Concord’s classes were photographed each year on the stair of the main entrance.

The prints in the district archive are numbered in white ink and dated by hand, and together they form a record of the neighbourhood’s children across eight decades.

The earliest surviving portrait shows the entire school assembled at once. Pupils fill the stair and the ground on either side of it, the youngest seated in front, the oldest standing on the stair walls, with members of the baseball team in their lettered jerseys among them. The portraits of the 1930s show single classes: boys in overalls and lace-up boots, girls in cotton print dresses and, in winter, heavy coats.

South Park at that time was a community of small farms and houses, home to families of Italian, Japanese and other backgrounds. Japanese American farmers in the Duwamish and Green River valleys supplied a large share of the produce sold at Pike Place Market, although state law prevented them from owning land.

In 1942, under Executive Order 9066, Japanese American families in the region were forcibly removed and incarcerated. Many did not return. The class portraits from the preceding years include some of their children.

The same stair, in colour

The class portraits continued into the 1990s. The setting had not changed: the photographer still worked at the main entrance, and pupils still stood on the stair between the same stone walls, the smallest in front. The prints were now in colour, mounted on card with the school year, the teacher’s name and the names of every pupil.

The 1996-97 school year was a significant one for Concord. That autumn it became an ESL and bilingual centre, with about 17 per cent of its pupils receiving bilingual services. By then South Park was home to a large Latino community.

In 1999 the school closed for renovation. When it reopened in September 2000, the entrance and its stair, designated a city landmark the year before, had been preserved unchanged.

04 Chapter 4 Inside the school

The lunchroom and the blackboard

Inside the school, 1940s to 1990

A photograph from the Seattle Post-Intelligencer collection shows Concord’s lunchroom at a busy midday: long wooden tables and benches, glass milk bottles and plates of food, with staff in aprons serving at the far end of the room.

Enrolment rose sharply after the Second World War. It reached its peak of 516 pupils in 1958-59, and by 1960 six portable classrooms stood on the grounds. In 1971 the district added a gymnasium and multipurpose room, designed by Shavey & Schmidt, and the number of portables was reduced to three.

By the early 1970s a large mural covered the lunchroom’s end wall: concentric rings, clouds and stars, plants and fruit, and at its centre a sun with a face. In one photograph of the period, a hand-lettered sign beside the flag warns that the paint is still wet.

The 1938 blackboard

In 1990 workers removing a chalkboard to install bulletin boards found an older blackboard behind it. It had been decorated by the sixth-grade class of June 1938 with three coloured-chalk drawings of scenes from Norway, Portugal and Italy. The six artists signed beneath their pictures, and the other 20 members of the class signed in lists between them.

The discovery led to a reunion of the teacher, Mary Luch Bleakney, and several of her former pupils.

05 Chapter 5 Renovation

Repair, not replacement

Landmark status and the 1999 drawings

By the 1990s the building needed substantial work.

Seismic upgrades were carried out in 1992, but the building systems were ageing and the school needed more space. As with many schools of its period, the district faced a choice between renovation and replacement.

On 18 February 1998 the City of Seattle designated the 1913 exterior and the first-floor entrance stairwell as a landmark. The renovation that followed was funded by the district’s first Building Excellence levy and designed by the Tsang Partnership.

The record drawings, dated February 1999, set out what would be removed and what would be kept. Interior partitions, finishes and ceilings were to be demolished. Structural beams and columns, concrete floors, stairs and masonry walls were to remain. The three roof ventilators were to be refurbished, the masonry cleaned and decayed wood sash replaced to match the original.

A new classroom wing and gymnasium were built beside the original in banded masonry block. Pupils spent the 1999-2000 school year at the E. C. Hughes building and returned in September 2000. The district also purchased the remaining houses on the block, bringing the whole of it into school ownership.

06 Chapter 6 Today

Two languages, one hill

Concord International School

Industry now surrounds much of South Park, and a freeway separates Concord from part of the neighbourhood it serves.

In 2010 the 1931 South Park Bridge over the Duwamish was closed as unsafe, leaving the community without its nearest river crossing until a replacement opened in 2014.

The school’s programmes changed with its community. Concord became an ESL and bilingual centre in 1996-97. From 1997 it was a model school for the Different Ways of Knowing programme, which combined social studies and the arts, and its Attic Theater programme brought all pupils into drama, dance and cultural studies. As part of a world cultures programme, pupils once built a model Mayan village.

In 2009 Concord introduced an International School programme with two-way dual-language immersion in Spanish and English, the third such programme in Seattle. Enrolment reached 412 in 2014, when 49 per cent of pupils received bilingual services.

The emblem

The school’s emblem is a bold letter C divided into five segments, each containing a cut-paper figure: a bird, a branching plant, a winding river, a creature and an open book. Two versions hang beside the main entrance, below the name carved in 1913.
